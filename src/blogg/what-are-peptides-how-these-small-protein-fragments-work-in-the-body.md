Not long ago, [peptides ](https://peptideslux.com/)were something you came across in a biology textbook. Today the word is printed on tubs of collagen powder and on pricey face serums, and it pops up in videos where people talk about faster recovery and bigger muscles. It is easy to come away thinking peptides are a new discovery. They are not. Your body has been making them all along, and some of our most important medicines are peptides.

So what are we actually talking about?

## A peptide is a short chain of amino acids

Every protein in the body is built from amino acids, about 20 different kinds that can be combined in an almost endless number of ways. When two amino acids join together, they form what is called a peptide bond. Keep adding more and you get a chain.

If the chain is short, it is called a peptide. If it grows long and folds into a three-dimensional shape, it is called a protein. The line between the two is blurry, but a common rule of thumb is that anything up to about 50 amino acids counts as a peptide. Insulin, with its 51 amino acids, sits right on the border, which is why you will see it described as both.

A few examples show how much the size can vary:

- **Carnosine**, found in muscle, is made of just two amino acids.
- **Glutathione**, one of the body's most important antioxidants, is made of three.
- **Oxytocin**, the hormone involved in labour and breastfeeding, among other things, is made of nine.
- **GLP-1**, a hormone released from the gut when you eat, is made of just over 30.

## What do peptides do in the body?

Many peptides act as messengers. They are released in one part of the body and attach to receptors on cells somewhere else, a bit like a key in a lock. Once the key is in place, the cell responds.

Insulin is the textbook example. When your blood sugar rises after a meal, the pancreas releases insulin, which binds to receptors on muscle and fat cells and tells them to take up sugar from the blood. GLP-1 works in a similar way but has several jobs at once: it boosts insulin release, slows the emptying of the stomach and signals to the brain that you are starting to feel full.

One thing that makes peptides unusual is how short-lived they are. The body's own GLP-1 is broken down in about two minutes. That is partly the point, because the signal should only be there when it is needed. But it also makes peptides tricky to use as medicines.

## Why many peptide medicines are injected

If you swallow a peptide, your stomach treats it like any other protein in your food and cuts it up into individual amino acids. That is why most peptide medicines are given as injections.

Insulin is the oldest example. It was first used to treat a patient with type 1 diabetes in 1922 and has saved millions of lives since. Today there is a whole group of medicines that mimic GLP-1, the best known being semaglutide. Researchers altered the molecule so that it is broken down more slowly and binds to proteins in the blood. As a result, its half-life went from a couple of minutes to roughly a week, which is why a single injection once a week is enough.

There is also a tablet version of semaglutide, but it needs an added ingredient to protect the molecule in the stomach, and only a small share of the dose is absorbed.

## Collagen peptides: what does the research say?

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the body and is found in skin, tendons, cartilage and bone. Collagen peptides are collagen from sources such as fish or cattle that has been broken down into smaller pieces so it dissolves and absorbs more easily.

A common objection is that the body breaks collagen down into amino acids anyway, so it should not matter where the protein comes from. That is partly true. But studies have shown that some very short peptides, made of two or three amino acids, can be absorbed intact and turn up in the blood after a dose. Whether they then have an effect on the skin is still not fully understood.

A 2021 review that pooled 19 studies with just over a thousand participants found that collagen supplements led to some improvement in skin hydration and elasticity. The effects were modest, and many of the studies were funded by manufacturers. In short, there is some support, but nowhere near the dramatic results that advertising sometimes suggests.

## Peptides in skincare

Peptides are a common ingredient in face serums. Often these are so-called signal peptides, such as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, which in lab tests has been shown to stimulate skin cells to produce collagen. Another group is copper peptides such as GHK-Cu, a peptide that occurs naturally in the blood and has been studied for wound healing.

The problem is that much of this research has been done on cells in a dish or in small, short studies. A peptide also has to get through the outermost layer of the skin to do anything, and that is not a given. Peptides in skincare are generally gentle, but it pays to keep your expectations realistic.

## The bottom line

Peptides are short chains of amino acids and a completely natural part of the body. They often act as messengers and control everything from blood sugar to fullness to childbirth. Several of them have become important medicines, and insulin has been in use for more than a hundred years.

Collagen peptides and peptides in skincare have some backing from research, but the effects are modest. Peptides injected outside of medical care are a different matter: the evidence is often limited and the rules are clear.

So the next time you see the word peptide on a label or in a video, it is worth asking two questions: which peptide is it, and what does the research say about that particular one?
